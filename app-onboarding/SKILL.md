# App Onboarding

Use this skill to design onboarding as the first product experience: a short narrative that makes users feel understood, committed, and ready for the app's core promise before asking for signup, review, trial, or payment.

## Core Principle

Do not treat onboarding as "here are the features." Treat it as a story:

1. Show the user you understand their problem.
2. Make them reflect on why it matters.
3. Build emotional commitment.
4. Position the app as the obvious solution.
5. Ask for signup, review, trial, or payment at the right moment.

The flow needs an intro, a climax, and a conclusion. Screen order matters.

## Design Workflow

### 1. Define the conversion job

Before writing screens, identify:

- The user's cold-install state: what anxiety, job, trigger, or hope brought them here.
- The app's promised transformation: what the user should believe by the end.
- The conversion ask: signup, permissions, trial, paywall, review, or purchase.
- The proof needed before that ask: demo value, personalization, social proof, privacy assurance, or cost transparency.

Aim for the user to think: "Yes, this is exactly for me."

### 2. Build a story arc

Use this default sequence unless the product has a stronger reason to differ:

```txt
Problem recognition
-> Personal questions
-> Reflection
-> Promise/result
-> Social proof
-> Small commitment
-> Signup, review, trial, or paywall
```

Move monetization and reviews later unless the app has already created value or commitment. By the time the user sees the conversion ask, they should already believe:

```txt
This app understands me.
This problem matters.
This app can help.
People like me use it.
```

### 3. Use questions to create commitment

Questions are not only for personalization. Use them to make users state their own motivation.

Strong question bank pattern:

```txt
What are you struggling with?
-> I want to feel more prepared

How important is this to you?
-> Very important

What happens if you do not solve it?
-> I will feel lost, behind, or uninvolved
```

Prefer questions about struggle, importance, consequence, identity, desired outcome, and current stage. Avoid generic preference questions unless their answers visibly change the experience.

### 4. Reflect answers back

After important answers, insert a reflection screen instead of immediately moving on.

```txt
Got it. You want simple weekly updates without medical overload.
```

Later, mirror the user's own stated motivation:

```txt
Based on your answers, Dady will help you feel more prepared and involved week by week.
```

Reflection screens make simple branching feel personal and make the user feel heard.

### 5. Demonstrate one useful result

Before asking for payment or account creation, show a concrete preview of value when possible:

- A personalized weekly insight.
- A checklist item or recommended next action.
- A simulated message, report, or result.
- A sample question the user should ask.
- A progress or readiness summary based on answers.

Keep the preview specific to the user's answers, even if the logic is lightweight.

### 6. Place asks intentionally

Use this timing guidance:

- Signup: after the user sees why saving their personalized setup matters.
- Permissions: after explaining the immediate benefit of that permission.
- Review prompt: after a satisfying value moment, not on first launch.
- Trial or paywall: after problem recognition, personal commitment, proof, and a preview of value.
- Payment for expensive features: explain the cost honestly and connect it to the unlocked value.

## Screen Blueprint

When delivering an onboarding design, provide a screen-by-screen flow. For each screen include:

- Screen goal.
- Headline and body copy.
- Primary action and secondary action, if any.
- Question options or input fields.
- Reflection or branching logic.
- Why the screen belongs at this point in the story.

Keep copy emotionally specific and concise. Avoid feature-list language unless it directly supports the user's stated problem.

## Example Patterns

### Pregnancy tracker for dads

```txt
1. "Most pregnancy apps were not built for dads."
2. "How involved do you want to be during the pregnancy?"
3. "What is hardest right now?"
   - Understanding what is happening
   - Knowing what to ask
   - Remembering important dates
   - Supporting my partner
4. "Got it. We will keep you updated without medical overload."
5. Ask due date or current week.
6. Show baby size comparison.
7. Show one useful weekly insight.
8. Show "what to ask this week."
9. "Built for dads who want to be involved without becoming doctors."
10. Show trial or paywall for costly AI/premium features.
```

### Car-to-car messaging app

```txt
1. "Someone blocked you. What do you do?"
2. "Instead of honking, waiting, or calling police, message the car."
3. Enter plate.
4. Show simulated outgoing/incoming message.
5. Explain privacy, reports, and blocking.
6. Ask for permission or signup.
```

## Review Checklist

Before finishing, check:

- The flow starts from the user's problem, not the app's feature list.
- Each question increases personalization, commitment, or both.
- Important answers are reflected back to the user.
- The user receives at least one concrete value preview before a major ask.
- Social proof appears before the paywall when trust is needed.
- Signup, review, trial, and payment prompts happen after a value or commitment moment.
- The final flow has a clear intro, climax, and conclusion.
- The design targets activation or conversion improvement, not just education.
